# Approaching the additional dehydrated fruit prospects

Use the new workbook's **First 30** sheet to begin. Each row links a prospect to its public contacts, likely application, buyer role and a practical next step. These priorities reflect location, product fit and contact evidence. They are not predicted conversion rates.

A useful outcome from the first conversation is a named buyer who confirms a relevant drink or recipe and agrees to a next step. A paid trial and a reorder establish demand more reliably than an attractive menu or a positive comment about a sample.

## Start with a small calling batch

Call five to ten prospects at a time, beginning with nearby beverage-led venues. Ask whether it is a convenient time for a one-minute supplier enquiry. Confirm the business name and branch before discussing products, especially for directory-sourced numbers. Ask when the decision-maker is available rather than interrupting service.

Use the workbook's **Approach Plans** sheet for the opening message and qualification question for each business. General support emails and reservation lines are routing contacts; ask for the owner, beverage manager, head bartender or ingredient buyer. Where a chain or group is flagged, ask whether the outlet can approve a local supplier before delivering samples.

An opening for a nearby café:

> Hi, I'm [your name] from Poranki. I'm setting up a small dehydrated-fruit business. Could I speak with the person who chooses ingredients for your drinks? I'd like to find out whether orange or lemon wheels would suit one of your iced teas or mocktails. If useful, may I arrange a small tasting at a convenient time?

Use this as an introduction to the specific question in that business's row. A published fruit-flavoured drink does not mean the buyer wants its dried form, so confirm the application before producing samples.

## Match the offer to the buyer

| Business | First conversation | Proposed trial | What makes the next step useful |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cocktail bar or brewery with a cocktail menu | Ask the head bartender how garnish is currently bought or made | Orange and lemon wheels, with lime separately specified if requested | One named drink, an accepted size and a target cost per usable piece |
| Mocktail, iced-tea or cooler café | Ask the owner or beverage lead which drink would benefit | A small orange and lemon comparison in the actual drink | The buyer confirms appearance, bitterness and willingness to pay |
| Coffee-led café | First establish whether iced teas, coffee tonics or other suitable drinks are actually served | One buyer-selected fruit and drink; avoid assuming garnish belongs on all coffee | A defined beverage application before dispatch |
| Bakery or dessert café | Ask the pastry chef about a named recipe and desired texture | A small recipe trial with one fruit; orange decoration or buyer-selected pineapple, apple or mango pieces | Accepted piece size, texture, ingredient declaration and portion cost |
| Delivery-only café | Call the operator and ask whether the garnish survives their delivery format | A sealed sample tested after a representative delivery journey | The buyer checks moisture, breakage and handling at the point of serving |
| Hotel or larger group | Ask F&B who approves garnish specifications and new vendors | A technical sample after the buyer shares requirements | A named buyer and a clear onboarding process |

These are suggested sample formats, not production commitments. Confirm sample size, any charge, timing and delivery address with the buyer. Produce samples using the same food-safety and batch controls you intend for commercial supply. The [launch plan](launch_plan.md) covers product readiness and equipment validation.

## Personalize the message without inventing facts

Use the business's name, branch and an evidence-backed menu cue. If the only evidence is a search listing, phrase the cue as a question and confirm the current menu. Keep the proposed use distinct from what the business already does.

**Subject:** Small citrus garnish trial for [business name]

Hi [name or team],

I'm [your name], based in Poranki, and I'm setting up a dehydrated-fruit offering for cafés and bars.

Would orange or lemon wheels be relevant to any of your [confirmed drink type]? I'd like your beverage team's feedback on the size, appearance and flavour in one actual drink before discussing regular supply.

Who handles this purchase, and would a brief sample appointment be useful?

Thanks,
[your name]
[business phone]

For a bakery, replace the drinks paragraph with the buyer's requested recipe, fruit, piece size and texture. For a farther city, ask for the specification and sample agreement by phone or email before paying for shipping.

## Qualify the opportunity before quoting

Record these answers in **Contact Log**:

1. Who approves the product and purchase? Does the outlet have buying authority?
2. Which drink or food would use it? Is dried fruit wanted at all?
3. Does the business buy garnish, make it in-house, or use fresh fruit only?
4. What size, colour, texture, fruit variety and usable piece count are acceptable?
5. What is actual weekly use for that application, and what price basis does the buyer prefer?
6. What sample or document is required, and who will evaluate it?
7. What is the agreed next action and date?

Leave quantities, budget, buyer identity and interest blank until the business provides them. Use measured production costs and the approved specification before quoting. Do not imply that a smaller pack, local delivery or dried garnish automatically saves money; test that with the buyer's actual process.

## Follow up around a decision

After a sample, ask which drink or recipe was tested and what needs to change. If the sample is accepted, propose a small **paid trial** with fruit, pack quantity, usable piece count or weight, price, delivery charge, date and payment terms agreed in writing.

If there is no response, one concise follow-up after about four to seven working days is a reasonable starting routine. Ask whether to continue or close the enquiry. Respect a request to stop and record it in Contact Log. Use WhatsApp for specifications or photos after the recipient agrees to receive them.

For a current supplier, ask whether a backup option would be useful. For in-house drying, ask what is difficult about the current process and whether an external sample is worth testing. If the venue has no relevant recipe or no interest in paying for garnish, stop spending on samples there.

## Use the workbook as a working record

**New Leads** is the dated research snapshot. **Contact Log** is where you record actual contact, interest, samples, orders and follow-up. The two sheets do not synchronize automatically. **Sources** explains where contacts and menu cues came from, including search-index-only and directory evidence.

Track the number of businesses reached, buyers identified, sample agreements, accepted samples, paid trials and reorders. Your own results will show which segment converts for your product and price. The workbook starts with zero confirmed buyers and no sent messages.

Keep the original 50-lead workbook as a separate file. Rebuilding the expansion workbook replaces its generated sheets, so save a working copy before entering sales notes.
